# Campus Slot Lab

A campus salon operator has one chair and sixteen appointments in an afternoon. A student who planned ahead wants a confirmed booking. A student between classes needs a walk-in slot. How much capacity should the operator set aside for each?

**Try it:** download `index.html` and open it in a browser. It runs offline with no installation or account. Change demand, no-shows, or either access target, click **Compare policies**, then export the table as CSV. To check the model, use Node.js 18+ and run `node --test model.test.cjs`.

## A decision you can reproduce

With the defaults (16 requests, 8 expected walk-ins, 15% booking no-shows, seed 42), the operator wants to accept at least 70% of advance requests and serve at least 45% of walk-ins. Each policy runs on the same 200 synthetic four-hour shifts.

| Held slots | Visits per shift | Advance requests accepted | Walk-ins served |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 15.29 | 100.0% | 21.7% |
| 2 | 14.81 | 87.5% | 36.5% |
| 4 | 14.33 | 75.0% | 50.7% |
| 6 | 13.80 | 62.5% | 64.1% |
| 8 | 13.06 | 50.0% | 75.8% |

Four holds clear both targets. In the simulation that costs roughly one completed visit per shift compared with zero holds, while raising walk-in service by 29 percentage points. With a 90% advance-access floor and the original walk-in target, none of the tested policies qualify. Try other seeds to see how close a policy sits to a threshold.

## Product decisions

- **Primary user:** a campus service operator choosing next week's mix of walk-ins and appointments. Students experience the consequences of that decision. These are design assumptions for an independent prototype.
- **Objective:** maximize completed visits while meeting explicit access targets for both groups. A throughput-only objective would recommend zero holds in the default scenario.
- **First release:** a single file with a reproducible scenario and export. The operator can review policy options without collecting student identities. Direct booking and staffing optimization would require different workflows and data.
- **Model:** each advance request takes the earliest available non-held slot. Holds are spread across the shift. Walk-ins follow a constant Poisson arrival rate and queue first-come, first-served. A no-show releases capacity at the beginning of its slot. One visit occupies one slot, and each shift starts empty. The simulation reuses demand across policies to reduce randomness in comparisons.
- **Limits:** actual service times, class-break rushes, late arrivals, and customer willingness to accept a particular time can change the decision. The served-only wait percentile excludes students who leave. View it alongside the walk-in service rate.

## Proposed field test

Ask an operator and several students to walk through the busiest hour of their last visit. Check whether appointment refusals, arrivals, waiting, and walkaways can actually be counted. Record only aggregate counts and anonymous timestamps with the operator's agreement. Start with observation; the assumptions above have not been validated with users.

If the operator agrees, compare the incumbent policy and the suggested policy across matched weekday shifts in alternating order. Track requests, bookings, walk-in arrivals, walkaways, completed visits, wait times, and staff overtime. Review the two access thresholds with the operator before starting. Pause if either group loses access beyond an agreed limit. Treat low-volume or inconsistent results as inconclusive and extend the pilot before adopting a schedule.

## Research basis

Creemers, Lambrecht, Beliën and Van den Broeke evaluate scheduling rules across waiting time, idle time and overtime. Their framework motivates using several service measures. The parameters and numerical results above are generated by this prototype. [Evaluation of appointment scheduling rules (Omega, 2021)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.omega.2020.102231)
